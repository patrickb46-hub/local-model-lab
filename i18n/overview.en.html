Can a small AI student surpass its teacher?

Local Model Lab · Distillation vs. Reinforcement Learning, a controlled experiment

October 2026 ｜ Every step run CPU-only on a 2018 Intel MacBook Pro ｜ Reproducible with no internet connection

The verdict up front: yes. On questions "the teacher itself can't solve", a small model trained by practicing on its own (reinforcement learning) reaches 56%, while one trained by copying the teacher's worked solutions (distillation) not only fails to improve, but falls back from 40% to 17%.

1. What this is about

Let's use an analogy. There's a small student (a small AI model) who needs to practice math. Beside them is a big student (a larger AI model), who is more advanced.

Now there are two ways to practice:

Method 1 · Distillation

Copy the big student's worked solutions

The small student copies, step by step, how the big student solves each problem and how they start writing it down. After enough copying, the small student's thinking starts to look like the big student's.

Method 2 · Reinforcement Learning

Solve problems yourself, check your own answers

Nobody teaches them. The small student solves problems, then checks the answers; get it right and remember the approach, get it wrong and adjust. Dozens of rounds.

Method 3 · Copy, Then Practice

Copy once, then solve problems yourself

Use both routes: first copy the big student's worked solutions as a foundation, then practice on your own.

These three ways of practicing map to the project's three experimental subjects: A1 (distillation), B (reinforcement learning), C (distillation first, then reinforcement learning). There are also two reference points: base (the original small student, trained on nothing) and teacher (the big student itself).

Why not just train "the big student" directly? Because this machine has only 16GB of RAM and no discrete GPU — training the big student (9B parameters) is physically impossible. So we only train the small student, make it as strong as we can, and see whether it can step up.

2. The key design: splitting the questions into three tiers

This is the single most important design decision in the whole experiment. The questions can't be chosen at random — they have to come in three tiers, so that you can tell whether something was truly learned or just copied.

L1
Questions the big student can also solve (basics) 20 questions. Used to check whether the fundamentals have slipped.
20 questions
L2
Questions the big student itself can't solve either ← this tier matters most 16 questions. The original small student actually gets a few of them right.
16 questions
L3
Questions neither the big student nor the original small student can solve 16 questions. Used to test whether a genuinely new skill was learned.
16 questions

Why does L2 matter most? Because if you only compare on "questions the big student can do", the copycat is bound to win — those are exactly the ones they copied. Only on questions the big student itself can't solve can you tell whether someone really thinks, or only imitates.

These questions aren't picked at random — they're math problems generated automatically by a script, each with a single correct answer that is 100% easy to grade — so there's no "disputed grading criteria" problem, and the results are completely objective.

3.3 Another angle: from basics to hard questions, who's falling off?

Line the three tiers up in order of difficulty and follow how each role trends across them — a line chart is more intuitive than a table: the ones that should stay flat stay flat, the ones that should drop do drop.

How to read this chart: moving right along the horizontal axis, the questions get harder. A healthy model should "decline slowly"; but the A1 line falls off a cliff in the middle (53% → 17%), which shows it only learned what the teacher had taught it. Once the teacher can't do it either, A1 fails completely — and on top of that it was dragged off course by the teacher's wrong approach. By contrast, the B and C lines decline far more gently.

3.4 One more surprise: from 0 to 25% on L3

On the hardest tier — the questions "neither the big student nor the original small student can solve": the original small student is at 0%, while the two that practiced on their own reached 20.8% and 25.0%.

This shows they didn't get there by imitation — no one had ever solved these questions, so there was nothing to imitate. They worked out, on their own, problem-solving ability the original simply doesn't have.

Summary in four lines

  • Copying helps on "questions the teacher can do": 53.3% beats the 48.3% of not studying, but only by a little.
  • Copying hurts on "questions the teacher can't do": it drops from 39.6% to 16.7% — it copied the teacher's mistakes along with everything else.
  • Practicing on your own is the real skill: on questions the teacher can't solve, it reaches 56.2%, almost 1.5 times "not studying".
  • And it was "learned", not "copied": on the hardest tier it went from 0% to 20%+.

3.5 Are these gaps real? — Statistical testing

The numbers differ, but could it just be luck? We ran a paired test (comparing, question by question, who got each one right and who got it wrong):

Comparisonp-valueConclusion
Copying vs practicing on your own (A1 vs B) 0.0018 significant — practicing on your own is clearly better
Practicing on your own vs teacher (B vs teacher) 0.0309 significant — the student overtook the teacher
Copy then practice vs teacher (C vs teacher) 0.0309 significant — the student overtook the teacher
Copying vs teacher (A1 vs teacher) 0.7266 not significant — copying did not overtake the teacher

A p-value below 0.05 is usually taken as "unlikely to be luck". The top three rows pass, the last one doesn't — and that's exactly the dividing line we were looking for.

For honesty's sake, two things must be said:
① This tier has only 16 questions, so the sample is on the small side. The direction is very clear and the statistical test did reach "significant", but stronger evidence would need the question pool enlarged 2–3 times and the study redone.
② The top cloud model (DeepSeek V4 Pro) scores 97.9% on this tier. So although the small model overtook its own teacher, it is still very far from a truly strong model.

4. How the whole plan unfolded

From first step to conclusion, about 40 hours of machine time, split into two big rounds.

Step 1 · Question generation and screening Automatically generate math problems → have the big student and the original small student each solve them 5 times → split into L1/L2/L3 by "who can and who can't" (about 7 hours — the most time-consuming step)
Step 2 · Lock the exam Freeze the 52 questions into an "exam" that no training is allowed to touch afterward — to keep the test fair
Step 3 · Train A1 (copied the big student) 1 hour 27 minutes
Step 4 · Train B (practiced on its own) 3 hours 50 minutes — the slowest step, because each question had to be solved 8 times on the spot before summarizing
Step 5 · Train C (copied then practiced) 2 hours 30 minutes
Step 6 · The uniform exam 6 roles × 52 questions × 3 attempts each = 936 responses, about 3.5 hours total
Step 7 · Produce the report Automatically compiled into charts and conclusions

There was one mishap along the way — during the first exam, because of a programming oversight, only the basics (L1) made it into the exam, and neither of the two hardest tiers was tested at all. So we ran a make-up exam (about 1 hour 11 minutes), and only then had this complete set of results.

6. A few terms, explained in one sentence

Distillation Have the small model imitate the big model's output. Equivalent to "copying the worked solutions". What it learns is whether it looks alike.
Reinforcement learning Let the model try things on its own and adjust by how good the result is. Equivalent to "solving problems and checking your own answers". What it learns is whether it is right.
LoRA (adapter) Leave the original model untouched; just add a very small layer of adjustable parameters alongside it. Saves memory, and it's easy to swap in and out.
teacher / student teacher model / student model. Here the teacher is 9B and the student is 2B (more parameters usually means smarter).
Consistency How similar the answering style is to the teacher's. The homework-copier has especially high consistency (61%), but knowing how to do something isn't the same as getting it right.
Diversity Whether the same question gets the same answer every time, or different lines of thinking. Too low means rote memorization.
